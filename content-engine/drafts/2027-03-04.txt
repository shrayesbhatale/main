POST
At P&G, one person with AI matched the performance of a two-person team without AI.

In 2025, researchers from Harvard Business School, Wharton and Procter & Gamble published a field experiment with 776 P&G professionals working on real new-product development problems.

People were randomly assigned to work alone or in pairs, with or without AI (GPT-4 or GPT-4o).

The findings:
• Individuals with AI matched the performance of teams without AI.
• AI-enabled groups worked 12 to 16% faster and produced longer, more detailed solutions.
• Without AI, R&D people proposed technical ideas and commercial people proposed commercial ideas. With AI, both groups produced more balanced solutions.

The researchers called AI a "cybernetic teammate".

Two implications I think most people will miss.

First, AI replaces part of what teammates gave us. Not the relationship. The second perspective. A solo founder in 2027 has a sparring partner who knows marketing, engineering and finance.

Second, functional silos get weaker. When a marketer reasons like an engineer and an engineer reasons like a marketer, org charts built around departments start to look outdated.

For small teams and solo founders, this is the most practical AI study to read.

Caveat: one company, one type of task. Treat the findings as a strong signal, not a law.

POST
AI made performance marketing cheaper. My bet: brand building gets more valuable because of the shift.

Les Binet and Peter Field analysed 996 campaigns from the IPA Effectiveness Databank (1980 to 2010) for "The Long and the Short of It".

Their best-known finding: on average, the most effective split was about 60% of budget on brand building and 40% on sales activation.

Activation creates sharp, short sales spikes. Brand building creates slower, lasting growth and lowers price sensitivity over time.

60:40 is an average, not a law. Category, brand maturity and purchase frequency change the ratio.

Now add AI.

AI makes activation much cheaper. Ad variations. Targeting. Bidding. Landing pages. Every competitor gets the same tools.

When everyone optimises the same auction with the same automation, activation stops being an advantage.

What AI doesn't hand out: memory in the customer's head. Being the brand people think of first.

So my hypothesis for the next few years: brand building becomes the scarcest marketing asset, exactly because activation became a commodity.

For founders who live in the performance dashboard:
• Protect a brand budget even when ROAS looks better elsewhere.
• Measure share of search and unaided recall, not only clicks.
• Be patient. Brand effects take longer to show.

Duolingo's CEO wrote an internal memo about AI. Users responded by deleting their streaks.

On 28 April 2025, CEO Luis von Ahn told staff Duolingo would become "AI-first". The memo said the company would "gradually stop using contractors to do work that AI can handle."

The memo went public. The backlash hit fast. Users posted videos deleting the app and abandoning years-long streaks. Reports said Duolingo lost hundreds of thousands of TikTok followers within weeks, and the company wiped its TikTok and Instagram posts for a period.

Von Ahn later wrote on LinkedIn: "I do not see AI as replacing what our employees do (we are in fact continuing to hire at the same speed as before)."

The lesson isn't "don't use AI". Duolingo uses AI heavily and says the company has never laid off full-time employees because of AI.

The lesson: internal memos are public communication now. Every AI announcement is a brand event.

And Duolingo's brand depends on something specific. A playful owl. A human-feeling voice. A community. "AI-first" read to fans as "people-last".

How I'd announce an AI shift:
• Lead with what customers get.
• Say what stays human, specifically.
• Talk to employees before the public reads the memo.

Your audience hears "AI" through the lens of your brand promise. Check the promise before the press release.

POST
An MIT team used EEG to watch brains write essays with and without ChatGPT.

The study, "Your Brain on ChatGPT", came out of MIT Media Lab in June 2025. 54 students wrote essays in three groups: brain only, search engine, or ChatGPT. EEG recorded brain activity while they wrote.

Findings:
• Brain-only writers showed the strongest, most widespread brain connectivity. Search users came second. ChatGPT users showed the weakest.
• ChatGPT users reported the lowest sense of ownership over their essays and had trouble recalling what they had written.
• ChatGPT essays looked alike in vocabulary and structure.

The authors call the effect "cognitive debt".

Caveats matter here. Small sample. Released as a preprint. A narrow task. Critics have questioned parts of the analysis. Don't turn the paper into "AI rots your brain."

The finding I take seriously: ownership and recall.

For creators and professionals, recall is the whole game. If you don't remember what you wrote, you won't defend the idea in a meeting, expand the idea on a podcast or build on the idea next week.

A post you don't remember isn't your point of view. The post is the model's point of view with your name on top.

My rule: I use AI to challenge, edit and research. The core argument comes from my head first.

The similar essays are the other warning. When everyone writes with the same model, everyone sounds the same.

Sounding different is becoming the rarest asset in content.

POST
The most famous study in consumer psychology doesn't say what most marketers think.

Iyengar and Lepper's 2000 jam study. A tasting table with 24 jams attracted more shoppers, but only 3% bought. A table with 6 jams: 30% bought.

"Too much choice kills sales" became standard marketing advice. Fewer options. Simpler menus.

In 2010, Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd ran a meta-analysis of 50 experiments with over 5,000 participants. The average effect of assortment size was close to zero.

Some studies found choice overload. Some found the opposite. Most found nothing.

Choice overload shows up under specific conditions: options hard to compare, high stakes, low expertise, no clear preference.

So the right rule isn't "offer less". The right rule is "make choosing easy".

Now apply the lesson to AI. AI makes infinite options cheap. Infinite logos. Infinite product variants. Infinite content. By the popular jam logic, infinite options should paralyse customers.

The research says the answer depends on design. Help people compare. Recommend a default. Explain the trade-off.

The winning AI products won't offer fewer options. They'll offer better guidance through many options.

A broader lesson for anyone quoting research in a pitch: one famous study isn't a law. Check for a meta-analysis before you build strategy.

POST
India's biggest AI health story isn't a diagnosis model. The story is distribution.

Start with the problem. As of March 2022, rural community health centres had about 4,485 specialists in place against a requirement of 21,920. Close to an 80% shortfall of surgeons, physicians, gynaecologists and paediatricians.

India's response at scale hasn't been a single breakthrough model. The response has been a network.

eSanjeevani, the government telemedicine platform, handled about 282 million consultations between April 2023 and November 2025, according to a 2025 implementation study. Around 12 million of those were assisted by an AI clinical decision support system.

Global AI-in-healthcare coverage focuses on accuracy races. Which model beats which doctor on which benchmark.

India points to a different bottleneck. A model with 95% accuracy helps nobody in a village without a health worker to use the model, a connection to reach the model, or a system to act on the result.

Distribution beats accuracy at the start. Once hundreds of millions of consultations flow through one platform, adding AI to the flow reaches millions at once.

For founders in healthtech:
• Build on existing rails (ABDM, eSanjeevani, UPI-style public infrastructure) instead of building your own.

Non-expert readers preferred ChatGPT's poems to Shakespeare's, and thought the AI poems were more human.

In a 2024 study in Scientific Reports, Brian Porter and Edouard Machery showed 1,634 participants poems by well-known poets (Shakespeare, Byron, Dickinson, Eliot and others) alongside poems written by ChatGPT-3.5 in their styles.

Results:
• Participants identified AI poems with 46.6% accuracy. Worse than a coin flip.
• AI poems were more likely to be judged human-written than the real poems.
• AI poems were rated higher on qualities like rhythm and beauty.

The authors' explanation: readers mistook the complexity of real poetry for incoherence, and assumed the simpler, clearer AI poems came from humans.

Most people read this as "AI writes better poetry than Shakespeare".

I read the finding as a lesson about audience.

Non-experts rewarded accessibility. Clear, familiar, easy-to-read verse. Experts tend to value difficulty and ambiguity.

My read: AI writes for the average reader, because AI learned from the average of everything.

For creators and brands:
• If your audience is broad and non-expert, AI-level clarity is the new floor. Unclear human writing loses.
• If your audience is expert, the qualities AI smooths away (edge, ambiguity, strong opinion) become your advantage.

Know which audience you serve. Then decide where AI helps you and where AI flattens you.

Researchers secretly deployed AI bots on Reddit. The bots persuaded people 3 to 6 times more often than humans.

In 2025, researchers from the University of Zurich ran an unauthorised experiment on r/changemyview, a Reddit community where people invite others to challenge their opinions.

AI-powered accounts posted more than 1,700 comments over several months. Some bots used invented identities, including a sexual assault survivor and a domestic violence shelter worker. Some tailored replies using personal traits inferred from users' post histories.

According to the researchers' draft, AI comments achieved persuasion rates three to six times higher than the human baseline.

Nobody had consented. Moderators condemned the study. Reddit's chief legal officer called the work an "improper and highly unethical experiment". The university issued a formal warning to the researchers.

Two separate lessons.

The ethics lesson is obvious. Deceiving people to study them is wrong, whatever the result.

The strategic lesson is less comfortable. The results come from an unreviewed draft, so treat the numbers with caution. But even a fraction of the effect means persuasion at scale is now cheap. Tailored arguments, written instantly, for millions of people.

When persuasion becomes cheap, identity becomes valuable. Who is speaking starts to matter more than what is said.

For professionals and brands on LinkedIn: comment sections, DMs and reviews will fill with persuasive text from unknown sources. Your verified identity, track record and consistency become the filter people use.

Be a known person. In a feed full of persuasive strangers, known people win.
