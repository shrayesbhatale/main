POST
People forgive a human's mistake faster than an algorithm's. Research also found the fix.

In 2015, Wharton researchers Berkeley Dietvorst, Joseph Simmons and Cade Massey published experiments on "algorithm aversion".

Participants watched a forecasting algorithm make errors. Afterwards they avoided the algorithm and chose a human forecaster, even though the algorithm outperformed humans in every study.

People lose confidence in algorithms faster than in humans after seeing the same mistake.

In 2018 the same team found a fix. When people were allowed to adjust the algorithm's forecast, even slightly, they became far more willing to use the algorithm. And their results improved.

Control, even a small amount, turned rejection into adoption.

This matters for everyone building AI products today.

Most AI products are black boxes with a "regenerate" button. The user accepts the output or throws the output away.

The research points to a third design:
• Let users edit and steer the output.
• Show a confidence level so errors aren't surprises.
• Give a small dial: tone, length, risk level.

People don't need a perfect AI. People need to feel in charge of an imperfect one.

My bet: the product with the best model will lose to the product with the best sense of control.

POST
India has 2 to 2.5 million active creators. Only 8 to 10% monetise effectively.

BCG's 2025 report on India's creator economy has a gap worth staring at:
• 2 to 2.5 million active creators (1,000+ followers).
• Creators influence over 30% of consumer decisions, shaping $350 to 400 billion of spending.
• Only 8 to 10% of creators monetise effectively.
• Direct creator revenue: $20 to 25 billion today, projected $100 to 125 billion by 2030.

Globally, Goldman Sachs estimated about 4% of creators earn more than $100,000 a year.

Influence is massive. Income is thin.

Most people blame the algorithm. I think the problem is structure.

A creator influences a purchase. The brand, the marketplace and the platform capture the margin. The creator gets a one-time fee.

The creators who break out stop selling influence and start owning a piece of the transaction:
• Their own product, even a small one.
• Affiliate or revenue share instead of flat fees.
• An owned channel (email, a WhatsApp community) no algorithm controls.

Influenced spending and creator revenue are different measures. But the direction is clear: creators capture a small share of the value they move.

My hypothesis: the next wave of Indian creator businesses will be built by creators who close the gap.

POST
The more people trusted AI, the less critical thinking they reported doing.

Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon surveyed 319 knowledge workers who use generative AI at least weekly. They collected 936 real examples of AI use at work. The paper appeared at CHI 2025.

Two findings pull in opposite directions:
• Higher confidence in AI was associated with less critical thinking.
• Higher confidence in yourself was associated with more critical thinking.

Important caveat: self-reported survey data. Association, not proof of cause.

Still, the pattern matches what I notice in practice. People with deep expertise use AI as a sparring partner. People without expertise use AI as an authority.

Same tool. Opposite relationship.

The paper also describes how critical thinking shifts with AI. Less effort on producing. More effort on verifying, integrating and stewarding the output.

The job doesn't disappear. The job moves from writer to editor.

And editing needs a standard. You judge AI output only against what you already know.

The rule I use: form my own view first, even a rough one. Then ask the AI. Then compare.

The order protects the thinking.

POST
In 2018 Naval called code and media "permissionless leverage". In 2026 the last gate fell.

On 31 May 2018, Naval Ravikant posted his "How to Get Rich (without getting lucky)" thread.

One line has aged better than most: "Code and media are permissionless leverage."

Labour needs people to follow you. Capital needs someone to give you money. Code and media need nobody's approval.

But in 2018 code still had a gate. You had to learn to program.

By 2026, AI coding tools let non-programmers ship working software from plain-language instructions.

So both forms of permissionless leverage now sit behind one skill: clear thinking, written in plain language.

A clear writer now builds tools. A clear thinker now publishes daily.

Here's the catch.

When leverage is available to everyone, leverage stops being an edge. Everyone ships apps. Everyone posts.

The edge moves to what AI doesn't hand out:
• Taste: knowing what's worth building.
• Distribution: people who already trust you.
• Judgment: knowing when output is wrong.

Leverage multiplies. Multiplying zero still gives zero.

Build the thing worth multiplying first.

Everyone is predicting 2027. Bezos asked a better question back in 2012.

At AWS re:Invent in 2012, Jeff Bezos said people often ask him what will change in the next 10 years. Then he added:

"I almost never get the question: 'What's not going to change in the next 10 years?'"

His point: build strategy on what stays stable. For Amazon's retail business, customers want low prices, fast delivery and vast selection. They'll want those in any decade.

December is prediction season. AI agents, new models, new platforms. Most predictions will age badly.

So here's my list for 2027. Things I'd bet stay the same no matter what AI does:
• People buy from people they trust.
• Attention goes to what feels specific and personal.
• Customers want problems solved faster, with less effort.
• Scarce skills get paid. Common skills get commoditised.
• Reputation compounds. So does a bad one.

In my view, every AI shift of the last three years pushed harder on these, not against them.

Before you plan around what's new, write down what won't change in your industry. Build on the list. Use AI to serve the list better.

Predictions expire. Constants compound.

A year ago, the head of Instagram argued platforms will need to prove what's real instead of catching what's fake.

On the last day of 2025, Adam Mosseri published a 20-slide year-end memo on AI and authenticity.

His core argument, paraphrased: AI content will become so common, verifying real media at the point of capture will be more practical than trying to catch every fake.

He also suggested creators lean into raw, unpolished, even unflattering content as a signal of being real.

One year later, the direction looks right to me.

Here's the shift for anyone building a brand.

Polish used to signal quality. Now polish is free. A model generates a flawless image in seconds.

When polish costs nothing, polish signals nothing.

Proof of reality becomes the premium:
• Behind-the-scenes over studio shots.
• Specific, dated, local details AI wouldn't invent.
• Your face, your voice, your mistakes.
• Consistency over years, which nobody fakes overnight.

The brands winning on social next year won't look the most professional. They'll look the most verifiably human.

Imperfection used to be a flaw. Now imperfection is evidence.
