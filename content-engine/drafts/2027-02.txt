POST
Two years ago this week, a "throwaway tweet" named a new way of building software.

On 2 February 2025, Andrej Karpathy posted: "There's a new kind of coding I call "vibe coding", where you fully give in to the vibes, embrace exponentials, and forget that the code even exists."

He later called the post "a shower of thoughts throwaway tweet". By November 2025, Collins Dictionary had named "vibe coding" its Word of the Year.

Two lessons. One about AI. One about branding.

The AI lesson: the phrase landed because the behaviour already existed. Many people were building software by describing what they wanted. Nobody had a word for the practice.

The branding lesson: naming a behaviour is leverage.

The person who names a trend gets tied to the trend for years. Karpathy didn't launch a product. He launched a label, and the label carried his name everywhere the idea travelled.

For anyone building a personal brand:
• Watch for behaviours people share but don't yet have words for.
• Give the behaviour a short, sticky, slightly playful name.
• Define the term clearly. Keep using the term.

Frameworks and names travel further than opinions.

One caution from two years of vibe coding: a working demo isn't production software. Security, maintenance and edge cases still need someone who understands the code.

POST
Microsoft's AI diagnosed 85.5% of hard cases. Physicians got 20%. Read the setup before you share the headline.

In June 2025, Microsoft AI released research on MAI-DxO, a system orchestrating several AI models to diagnose cases step by step, ordering tests and tracking cost along the way.

On 304 complex cases from the New England Journal of Medicine, MAI-DxO reached the correct diagnosis 85.5% of the time at its most accurate setting. 21 practising physicians from the US and UK averaged about 20%.

Four times better. The headline wrote itself.

The setup deserves as much attention as the score:
• The cases were NEJM teaching cases, chosen because they're hard and unusual. Real clinics see mostly common, mild problems.
• The physicians worked without colleagues, textbooks or the digital tools they'd normally use.
• No healthy patients were included, so false alarms weren't tested.

My take: the result is impressive and the comparison is unfair. Both are true.

The useful signal isn't "AI beats doctors". The useful signal is the design. Microsoft reported the orchestration approach improved results across model families from several labs. Several models questioning each other beat a single model.

The design lesson applies far beyond medicine.

Before you share any "AI beats experts" headline, ask three questions:
1. Which cases or tasks?
2. What were the humans allowed to use?
3. Which errors weren't measured?

POST
The "8-second attention span" statistic has no traceable research behind the number.

You've seen the claim in a hundred marketing decks. Humans have an 8-second attention span, shorter than a goldfish's 9 seconds.

The claim traces back to a 2015 Microsoft Canada consumer insights report. The report cited the figure from a website called Statistic Brain.

In 2017, BBC reporter Simon Maybin investigated. The sources Statistic Brain listed had no record of research supporting the numbers.

No study. No experiment. The goldfish figure has no traceable source either.

Yet the stat still shapes strategy. "Keep everything short. Nobody pays attention anymore."

Now look at behaviour. Multi-hour podcasts rank among the most popular shows. People binge whole seasons in a weekend. Long video essays pull millions of views.

My read: attention spans aren't shrinking. Tolerance for boring is.

People give unlimited attention to what feels worth attention, and zero to what doesn't. The feed made the decision faster. Not shorter.

What this means for creators and marketers:
• The first seconds decide whether someone chooses to stay. Earn the choice.
• After the choice, depth wins. Don't cut substance to fit a myth.
• Length isn't the enemy. Filler is.

And a broader lesson: before you build strategy on a statistic, find the original source. Many famous marketing numbers don't survive the search.

POST
Satya Nadella's test for AI isn't a benchmark. His test is economic growth.

In February 2025, on the Dwarkesh Podcast, the Microsoft CEO dismissed the race to declare AGI:

"Us self-claiming some AGI milestone, that's just nonsensical benchmark hacking to me."

His alternative: "The real benchmark is: the world growing at 10%."

Two years on, the line still gives the cleanest test for AI hype I know.

Benchmarks measure what a model does in a lab. Growth measures whether anyone's life or business changed.

The same logic scales down:
• For a company, AI's benchmark isn't the number of licences or pilots. The benchmark is margin, revenue per employee, time to serve a customer.
• For a creator, the benchmark isn't posts produced. The benchmark is audience trust and income.
• For a professional, the benchmark isn't prompts written. The benchmark is better decisions made.

Most AI dashboards today track activity. Usage. Adoption rate. Prompts per user. Activity is the easiest thing to measure and the least useful.

Pick one outcome metric you'd show a sceptical CFO. Track AI against the metric for a quarter.

If the number doesn't move, AI isn't working for you yet, no matter how impressive the demo.

POST
The top use of generative AI in HBR's 2025 analysis wasn't coding or writing. The top use was therapy and companionship.

The analysis, by Marc Zao-Sanders, was built from what people wrote on forums like Reddit and Quora. Not a random survey, but a strong signal of demand.

Two studies show why the topic needs nuance.

The encouraging one. Dartmouth's Therabot trial in NEJM AI (March 2025). 210 adults with depression, anxiety or eating-disorder risk. Therabot users showed significantly greater symptom reductions than a waitlist group, and rated their bond with the bot as comparable to a human therapist.

The cautionary one. OpenAI and MIT Media Lab (March 2025). A four-week randomized study with close to 1,000 participants, plus analysis of millions of conversations. Higher daily usage correlated with more loneliness, more emotional dependence and less socialising.

Both findings hold at once.

Therabot was purpose-built by clinicians, tested in a trial and supervised. General chatbots were built for everything.

My read: the demand is settled. People want someone to talk to at 2 a.m. The open question is design.
• Built to help you need the app less, or built to maximise time in the app?
• Clinically tested, or tested on engagement metrics?

Engagement is the default business model of consumer tech. For emotional support, engagement as the goal is the risk.

For founders in mental health: your north-star metric decides whether your product heals or hooks.
