Three numbers about AI and jobs. Only one of them is data.

1. The optimistic forecast. The World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025 projects 170 million jobs created and 92 million displaced by 2030. Net: +78 million.

2. The pessimistic warning. In May 2025, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei told Axios AI might wipe out half of entry-level white-collar jobs and push unemployment to 10 to 20% within one to five years.

3. The data. Stanford's payroll research, August 2026 update: no widespread, economy-wide displacement. But employment of 22 to 25 year olds in highly AI-exposed jobs sits about 19% below trend, mostly through fewer hires.

Two forecasts. One measurement.

The measurement sits between the extremes. And the measurement shows where the pain concentrates: early careers and codified-knowledge work.

Also note the WEF number most people skip. 170 million created plus 92 million displaced means churn of about 22% of formal jobs. A net positive number still means millions of people changing jobs, skills and cities.

My rule for 2027: read forecasts for direction. Read data for decisions.

And when someone quotes you a big AI jobs number, ask one question.

Forecast or measurement?

POST
After doctors got used to AI, their performance without AI dropped.

A 2025 study in The Lancet Gastroenterology & Hepatology looked at colonoscopies at four centres in Poland.

19 experienced endoscopists. Each had done more than 2,000 colonoscopies.

The centres introduced AI to help spot precancerous growths (adenomas). The researchers compared the doctors' detection rate in procedures done without AI, before and after the AI rollout.

Before: 28.4%.
After: 22.4%.

A 6-point absolute drop. About 20% in relative terms.

The limitations matter. Observational study. Few doctors. Patient groups differed between periods. Other experts have questioned the interpretation. Treat the result as a warning signal, not proof.

But the signal matches a pattern aviation has studied for years: heavy reliance on autopilot erodes manual flying skill unless pilots practise.

Every AI tool has an "off" state. Outages. Edge cases. Situations the model never saw. What matters then is the human skill left over.

For any team adopting AI:
• Schedule regular "no AI" reps on critical skills.
• Track human performance without the tool, not only combined performance.
• Keep your best people practising the hard part.

Efficiency with AI is visible.

Skill decay without AI is invisible, until the day you need the skill.

People value things more when they build them. AI products are removing the building.

In 2012, Michael Norton, Daniel Mochon and Dan Ariely published "The IKEA effect: When labor leads to love."

Across four studies (IKEA boxes, origami, Lego), people valued products they assembled themselves more highly. They saw their amateur creations as close in value to an expert's, and expected others to agree.

One boundary condition matters most today. The effect appeared only when people completed the task. When people built something and then destroyed the result, or failed to finish, the extra love disappeared.

Now look at how most AI products work. One prompt. Finished output. Zero effort.

Great for speed. Terrible for attachment.

If users contribute nothing, users feel no ownership. No ownership, weak loyalty. Switching to a competitor costs nothing emotionally.

Designs I think will keep users longer:
• Let users make meaningful choices, beyond typing a prompt.
• Build step by step so users feel the progress.
• Make the result feel like theirs: named, customised, saved.

Same for creators. Ask your audience to help build something (a framework, a series, a product) and they'll defend the result.

Effort isn't always friction.

Sometimes effort is the bond.

POST
Shopify's CEO changed who carries the burden of proof in hiring. Other companies will copy the change.

In April 2025, Shopify CEO Tobi Lütke posted an internal memo publicly, saying the memo was already in the process of leaking.

The key line: "Reflexive AI usage is now a baseline expectation at Shopify."

The memo also said teams asking for more headcount or resources must first show why AI won't get the work done. And AI use would become part of performance and peer reviews.

He asked teams: "What would this area look like if autonomous AI agents were already part of the team?"

Most coverage focused on the "AI or no hire" angle.

I think the bigger change is the burden of proof.

Before: a manager justified why a new hire was needed.

Now: the manager justifies why a machine won't do.

Once a well-known company flips the burden of proof, boards elsewhere start asking the same question. My prediction, not a fact yet.

What this means for professionals:
• Your value is the work AI won't do well. Know exactly what the work is.
• Show your AI workflow. Visible AI fluency is now part of performance.
• Document judgment calls, not only output.

What this means for founders: headcount is no longer a status symbol. Revenue per employee is.

POST
Healthcare professionals preferred ChatGPT's answers to doctors' answers 79% of the time. I don't buy the headline.

In 2023, researchers at UC San Diego took 195 real patient questions from Reddit's r/AskDocs, each answered by a verified physician.

They gave the same questions to ChatGPT (GPT-3.5).

A panel of licensed healthcare professionals compared the answers without knowing the author. They preferred ChatGPT's response 79% of the time. ChatGPT's answers were rated "empathetic" or "very empathetic" about 9.8 times as often.

The easy headline: "AI is more empathetic than doctors."

Look at one more number. The physicians' answers averaged about 52 words. ChatGPT's averaged about 211.

The doctors answered on Reddit in spare time. ChatGPT had unlimited patience and no waiting room. Empathy in writing takes time and words.

My read: the study largely measured who had time.

Which points to the real story. Healthcare doesn't have an empathy shortage. Healthcare has a time shortage.

The opportunity for AI in healthcare isn't replacing the doctor's empathy. The opportunity is returning the doctor's time:
• Drafting patient messages for the doctor to review.
• Handling documentation.
• Preparing patients before the appointment.

The study measured words on a screen, not health outcomes. Accuracy and safety need separate testing.

But patients want to feel heard. AI makes feeling heard cheap. Doctors should decide what to do with the time saved.

India banned 13 dark patterns in 2023. A 2025 audit found 97% of major platforms still using them.

In 2023, India's Central Consumer Protection Authority notified guidelines listing 13 dark patterns. Drip pricing. False urgency. Confirm shaming. Basket sneaking. Subscription traps. Nagging.

In June 2025, the CCPA told e-commerce and quick commerce platforms to self-audit and declare compliance within three months.

Meanwhile, a LocalCircles study between June and September 2025 found 97% of major online platforms still using manipulative design. Hidden fees. Drip pricing. False urgency.

Why do dark patterns survive regulation? Because dark patterns work, in the short term. Conversion dashboards reward them every day.

The cost shows up where dashboards don't look: trust.

The customer who finds a hidden fee at checkout doesn't file a complaint. The customer quietly uses a competitor next time.

Here's the risk ahead, as a hypothesis. AI personalisation makes dark patterns targeted. Urgency tuned to your anxiety. Prices tuned to your patience.

For founders, this opens a positioning gap. In a market full of tricks, radical honesty stands out:
• Show the full price upfront.
• One-tap cancel.
• No fake timers.

Honest design costs some conversion this quarter.

Trust compounds every quarter after.
