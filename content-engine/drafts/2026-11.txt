POST
MrBeast's videos lost money in 2024. His chocolate made money.

According to investor documents reported by Bloomberg in 2025, MrBeast's media business brought in roughly $250 million in 2024 and lost close to $80 million.

Feastables, his chocolate brand, brought in about the same revenue and earned more than $20 million in profit.

The most-subscribed creator on YouTube ran his content at a loss. The product paid the bills.

Most creators read this as "diversify your income".

I think the lesson runs deeper. The content was never the business. The content was the distribution.

MrBeast spends millions per video to earn attention. Attention costs money. A chocolate bar on a supermarket shelf turns attention into margin every time someone walks past.

Three takeaways for creators and founders:
• Treat content as customer acquisition, and judge the cost like customer acquisition.
• Own a product with margin. Ads and brand deals rent your audience to someone else.
• Pick a product people buy again and again. Chocolate, not a course bought once.

The creator economy talks about monetising content.

The winners monetise trust through a product, and let the content run at break-even or even a loss.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
POST
The riskiest AI failure isn't a wrong answer. The riskiest failure is a wrong answer from a tool you trust.

Harvard and BCG ran one of the most cited AI experiments to date. 758 BCG consultants. Realistic consulting work.

On tasks inside AI's capability, consultants using GPT-4 completed 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, with more than 40% higher quality.

On one task deliberately chosen to sit outside AI's capability, consultants using AI were 19 percentage points less likely to reach the correct answer than consultants without AI.

The researchers call the boundary a "jagged frontier". AI excels at some tasks and fails at neighbouring ones, and the line isn't where you'd guess.

Everyone quotes the 40%.

I think the 19 points matter more.

The consultants didn't fail because AI was bad. They failed because AI was good everywhere else, so they stopped checking.

Trust built on easy tasks carries over to hard ones.

For anyone managing a team using AI:
• Map your own frontier. List the tasks where AI output needs a full human check.
• Assign the checking to people with domain expertise.
• Reward people for catching AI errors, not only for speed.

The skill of the next decade isn't using AI. The skill is knowing where AI stops.

Coca-Cola doesn't sell soda at Christmas. Coca-Cola sells a memory.

Which explains the backlash to the company's AI-generated holiday ads, two years in a row, in 2024 and again in 2025.

The 2025 version was made with AI studios Silverside and Secret Level. Coke's head of generative AI told The Hollywood Reporter the "craftsmanship on this ad is ten times better" than the year before.

Better production didn't fix the reaction. Because the reaction was never about production quality.

People don't love the Coke holiday ad for the animation. They love the ad because the ad has run for decades and signals "the season has started". The ritual is the product.

Here's my framework for when AI production hurts a brand:
• Low risk: performance ads, product shots, variations, localisation. Nobody bonds with a banner.
• High risk: ritual and heritage moments, where people want proof of human effort.

AI reduces cost. In heritage moments, visible effort is part of the message. Effort signals care.

A rough rule: use AI for the 90% of content nobody remembers. Protect the 10% people remember.

AI helps beginners most. So why are companies hiring fewer beginners?

Two of the most solid AI productivity studies point the same way.

Customer support (Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2025): 5,179 agents. AI assistance raised issues resolved per hour by 14% on average. For novice and low-skilled agents: 34%. For the most experienced: minimal gains.

Professional writing (Noy and Zhang, Science, 2023): 453 professionals. ChatGPT cut time by 40% and raised quality by 18%. The weakest writers gained most.

The mechanism: AI packages what top performers know and hands the package to everyone.

AI compresses the skill gap. Beginners perform closer to experts.

Now the paradox. Stanford's payroll research shows fewer hires of 22 to 25 year olds in AI-exposed jobs.

If AI makes juniors almost as good as seniors, why hire fewer juniors?

My read: companies measure output, not learning. AI lifts a junior's output. AI also lifts a senior's output, and the senior already has judgment. In the short run the cheaper path to output wins.

The long-run cost arrives later, when nobody has learned the job the slow way.

If you're early in your career: use AI to speed up learning, not to skip learning. Ask the tool to explain, not only to answer.

If you lead a team: your best people's knowledge is now a transferable asset. Capture the knowledge on purpose.

POST
Doctors with GPT-4 scored 76%. Doctors without scored 74%. GPT-4 alone scored higher than both.

The study: a randomized trial in JAMA Network Open (Goh et al., 2024). 50 physicians. Complex clinical cases.

Half got GPT-4 plus their usual resources. Half used usual resources only.

Median diagnostic reasoning score:
• With GPT-4: 76%
• Without: 74%
The difference wasn't statistically significant.

Then the researchers ran GPT-4 alone. The model scored 16 percentage points higher than the physicians using conventional resources.

Headline version: "AI beats doctors."

I think the finding is stranger and more useful. Giving experts a strong tool barely moved their results. The tool alone beat the experts holding the tool.

Why? A few plausible reasons. Hypotheses, not findings:
• Doctors used GPT-4 like a search engine, not like a colleague.
• When the model disagreed with them, they trusted themselves.
• Nobody trained them to work with the tool.

The authors themselves point to technology and workforce development as the need.

The bottleneck in AI adoption, in healthcare and everywhere else, is the interface between an expert and the machine. Workflow. Training. Trust calibration.

Buying the tool is the easy part.

Teaching experts to change their minds when a tool is right is the hard part.

POST
In 2024 Klarna said AI did the work of 700 agents. In 2025 Klarna started hiring humans again.

February 2024: Klarna announced its AI assistant handled 2.3 million conversations in its first month. Two thirds of customer service chats. The equivalent work of 700 full-time agents.

May 2025: CEO Sebastian Siemiatkowski told Bloomberg: "As cost unfortunately seems to have been a too predominant evaluation factor when organizing this, what you end up having is lower quality."

Klarna then moved to a hybrid model and began recruiting human agents, so customers always have the option to reach a person.

Most commentary framed this as "AI failed".

I don't read the story as an AI failure. I read the story as a metric failure.

Klarna optimised support for cost. Customers judge support on one question: did my problem get solved, and did I feel respected?

When the metric is cost, AI wins every time. When the metric is trust, the answer depends on the problem.

A better design:
• AI for routine, low-stakes, high-volume questions.
• Humans for money disputes, complaints and anything emotional.
• A visible, fast path from bot to human.

Automation doesn't damage brands. Automation aimed at the wrong metric does.
